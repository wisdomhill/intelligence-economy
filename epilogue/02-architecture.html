The Architecture and the Shadow

Wisdom Hill Research

2026-10-08

4. The Architecture: Squeeze, Mesh, Substrate

Between the demand functions and the silicon stands the software industry, and the series devoted its longest arc — Reports 4 through 7 — to the architecture forming there. The arc has three movements: what is being squeezed, what is replacing it, and what it all stands on.

The squeeze. The SaaSpocalypse repricing — roughly $1.8 trillion of losses against nearly $1 trillion of gains across the thirty largest software companies in six months — was directionally right about the pressure and wrong about the mechanism (Report 4). Incumbents are not refusing agentic AI; every major vendor adopted the open protocols. What they built instead is openness at the protocol layer with closure rebuilt at the control and price layers: N competing tollgates, each declaring itself the registry, the meter, and the trust fabric. This defense collides with a new economic fact the series called the toll problem — for the first time in software history, the marginal user reads the price list. Agents touch software thousands of times a day and can be programmed to minimize cost per task in real time, so usage pricing set at human-era rates suppresses the very machine-scale traffic that consumption models require. The result is a squeeze theorem, not an apocalypse: hold the toll line and lose share, open to machine-scale pricing and lose margin — either branch impairs enterprise value, which is why the market de-rated franchises whose quarterly results continued to beat. The dividing line in the repricing was the denomination of revenue: companies paid per machine actor re-rated upward; companies paid per human seat de-rated, regardless of the quality of their AI announcements.

The mesh. The gridlock is itself the strongest force feeding the alternative. Report 5 traced a single causal chain — protocols, architecture, control, pricing, money — in which standardized interaction fabrics (API, MCP, A2A, and the emerging commerce layer) collapse the integration tax; enterprise software reorganizes into a distributed operating system in which applications are system calls, agents are processes, and routers are schedulers; power concentrates in the control plane, contested at four nested scopes from the department to the economy, with the industry scope — sector semantic authority — the most underpriced position in the stack; pricing resets from seats to settlements, transmitting inference COGS into software gross margins and ending the 80%-plus gross-margin identity of the SaaS era; and settlement itself migrates to machine-native dollars, because agents cannot hold bank accounts and sub-dollar machine payments are structurally impossible on card rails. The mesh is also the redemption of a twenty-year-old failure: the service-oriented architecture of 1999–2010 lacked exactly one component, a universal semantic interpreter, and large language models supplied it — in probabilistic form, which is why reliability engineering moved from compile-time contracts to runtime harnesses, and why the harness became a locus of differentiation valuable enough that the market paid multi-billion-dollar consideration for Manus, a harness company owning no frontier model (Reports 5 and 7).

The substrate. Beneath the mesh, the data layer’s fifteen-year 2×2 organization — structured versus unstructured, transactional versus analytical — collapsed along both axes under pressure from its first machine-speed customer, and value migrated upward into two new strata: the context layer that tells agents what data means, and the analytical reasoning layer where prediction, causal inference, and simulation become high-frequency agent workloads rather than scarce human projects (Report 6). Semantic lock-in emerged as the successor to data lock-in. And at the top of the substrate sits the office estate — the only horizontal software category, coextensive with the entire knowledge workforce — where the same migration takes its most consequential form: file formats are strategically spent, integration labor across document, spreadsheet, and presentation transfers to agents, and the endgame is the enterprise knowledge network, the organization’s knowledge as a curated, machine-executable estate from which documents, models, and decks are compiled on demand (Report 7). Across all three movements, the same asset kept surfacing under different names — accumulated context, the semantic layer, the context graph, knowledge capital — and the series’ claim is that these are one thing: the durable, non-transferable residue that remains after models, harnesses, and formats have all commoditized.

5. The Consumer Mirror, and a Second Front Still Being Sown

The consumer economy, examined in Reports 9 and 10, is not an appendix to the enterprise story; it is its controlled experiment. The enterprise reports argued that durable value forms at the control plane rather than the model layer. The consumer market validated the thesis in compressed time and at a different altitude: the consumer control plane formed at the operating system’s action-brokerage layer — Android’s AppFunctions shipped, Apple’s App Intents declared — exactly as the enterprise control plane formed at the tool and data gateway rather than the model (Report 9). The purest expression of the logic is the period’s strangest transaction: Apple licensing frontier intelligence for a reported billion dollars a year while retaining context, orchestration, and the action registry — the model treated as a component, like a display panel; the control plane treated as the franchise.

The consumer contest is really three: intent capture, action execution, and context ownership, each with its own competitive logic. The historic triumvirate of Google, Amazon, and Meta coexisted because each owned a different stage of the commerce funnel; agentic AI collapses the funnel into a single conversation, forcing each player to internalize the stages it does not own while defending its own. The period’s evidence ran in one direction: execution is harder than intent. OpenAI launched checkout inside the conversation and retired it within two months; Amazon, which owns catalog ground truth, payments, and fulfillment, moved up the funnel more credibly than any intent player moved down. The base case is accordingly not a winner but a layered partition — intent distributed, execution concentrated, and tolls collected at the OS duopoly’s action registries (Report 9).

Report 10 then opened the front the token lens cannot see, and closed Part One with the series’ widest frame: the two axes of context conquest. On the first axis — digital work context — the war is in its harvest phase: text-centric, verification-rich, monetized against labor, measured token by token. On the second — physical life context — it is in its sowing phase: generative media is the first monetizable beachhead, world models are the training infrastructure, and wearables are the sensor network, monetized against attention and commerce today and physical labor tomorrow.

6. Reading the Shadow: Two Demand Functions, Three Infrastructures

Part Two opens where the demand reports end: with a conversion problem. Token demand, Report 8 observed, is the shadow the enterprise sequence casts on the infrastructure market; GPU-hours are the shadow of the second front. Report 11’s mandate was to read both shadows onto silicon, and its central methodological claim is that the conversion cannot be performed onto a single hardware column. AI demand lands on three infrastructures, not one: the GPU/HBM complex that executes probabilistic inference, the CPU/DRAM complex that executes the deterministic harness and orchestration around it, and the Storage/NAND complex that absorbs the data agents generate and then re-consume through the analytical flywheel. A one-column model reads the shadow onto the wrong wall.

The quantified core: the combined AI inference fleet grows from roughly 1,203,000 H100-equivalents in 2026 to roughly 3,364,000 in 2028 — a 2.8-fold expansion — of which the B2B LLM track contributes 1,095,000 rising to 2,420,000 and the generative-media track 108,000 rising to 944,000. At a reference 1.35 kW per unit, that corresponds to on the order of 1.6 GW of facility load in 2026 and 4.5 GW in 2028. These are requirement figures for twelve core workloads — Report 8’s seven business functions and Report 10’s five media segments, treated as peers rather than as a token core with a media appendix — plus the image and audio tracks that serve the same media customers; they are a floor on modeled demand, not a forecast of accelerator sales, and because their scope is narrower than that of the installed base examined below, which also carries training, predictive-ML, consumer serving, and uncommissioned capacity, the series deliberately never states them as a proportion of it.

The decomposition matters more than the total, in two ways. The first is physics. LLM inference is dominated by autoregressive decode: memory-bandwidth-bound, gated by HBM capacity, stacking, and interconnect. Diffusion media generation is massive-batch dense computation: in the common case compute-bound, gated by raw FLOPS, power delivery, and cooling. The series named the industry’s growing practice of provisioning them separately the Great Decoupling — and was careful that the silicon buckets are not the demand tracks, since the batch-tolerant, budget-routed business functions are Token Track demand that lands on dense-compute silicon. On that cut, the HBM-bandwidth-favored core holds roughly 72% of the 2028 fleet against 28% for the dense-compute-favored segment, the latter expanding 8.8-fold over the window against 2.2-fold for the former, so its share rises from roughly 9% to roughly 28%. The Decoupling is thus a claim about increments, not convergence: the tracks do not approach parity inside the window, and dense compute’s rising share of marginal capacity is what carries the consequences — for the HBM-versus-logic mix of semiconductor demand, for power-density planning, and for which class of silicon supplier captures the 2028 margin.

The second is the identity of the giants, and here the decomposition overturns the intuition most capacity planning is built on. Two workloads dominate, on two different silicon tracks: software engineering at 934,000 H100-equivalents rising to 1,590,000, and — once the media track is opened into its segments — the monetized creator economy at 61,600 rising to 684,200, the second-largest individual workload in the model by 2028, while professional media and entertainment, the segment a casual reading would expect to dominate, requires 17,000. Together the coder and the creator account for roughly two-thirds of all AI inference silicon in the model. And the flow diverges from the stock: of the roughly 2,161,000 accelerators added between 2026 and 2028, generative media contributes about 836,000 against software engineering’s 656,000. The largest workload and the largest source of new demand are not the same function — which is the single most consequential fact in the series’ infrastructure arc for anyone provisioning capacity rather than describing it.

Two disciplines from this conversion deserve permanent residence in any reader’s toolkit, because violating either produces large errors with high confidence. The first is that token share is not compute share: because structured, low-wage tasks route to budget models whose per-token cost in FLOPs is five to ten times below frontier models, compute-weighted demand runs roughly 32–38% below the naive token total — approximately 13,700 trillion frontier-equivalent tokens in 2026 rising to 23,700 trillion in 2028 — so that an analysis converting nominal tokens to hardware with a single coefficient would overstate requirements by that margin. The margin is a scenario output of the model’s category weights, not a measured error band, and the frontier-equivalent series rather than the nominal one is what the infrastructure volume converts (Report 8). Report 11 then showed the naive conversion errs in both directions at once: it overstates by ignoring model mix, and understates by assuming benchmark throughput, since realistic serving for the dominant interactive workloads runs at roughly half the chat-style rate. The second discipline is the generalization, the four-distribution principle: token share, GPU share, CPU share, and storage share are four different distributions over the same functional taxonomy, and reading any one as a proxy for the others misreads the market in proportion to their divergence — the batch-tolerant business functions holding 4.7% of 2028 nominal tokens but 0.7% of the LLM accelerator fleet, while the judgment-heavy research function does the reverse and claims a larger share of frontier-model revenue still (Report 11). Both are instances of the series’ broader measurement discipline, which Part Two then applied to the installed base itself.

That application is Report 12, the series’ one purely empirical exercise, and its stock-flow distinction carries the strategic finding: in 2025 the accelerator installed base tripled, from 6.6 to 20.2 million H100-equivalents, and within that torrent Nvidia captured 79% of new dollar additions while losing share of the cumulative stock on both compute and cost metrics — eroded from two directions, hyperscaler in-house silicon and Chinese sovereign substitution, neither of which responds to merchant-market competition because neither is a merchant-market decision. The wedge behind the first force is the decade’s dominant capex fact: vertically integrated silicon delivering compute at a 2.4–5.7× per-unit cost advantage, tens of billions of dollars of avoided spend, and a 6.8-point one-year relative share swing between the integrated and merchant-dependent hyperscalers. China is the mirror image — the world’s most expensive compute, carrying a rough $22.5 billion export-control premium, substituting toward domestic supply at 85% of marginal additions regardless. Stock versus flow, here, is not an accounting nicety; it is the difference between the market the headlines describe and the market that exists.

7. The Inversion: Why the Cycle Turns While Demand Still Rises

Report 13 is the series’ theoretical center of gravity for Part Two, and its opening claim is deliberately jarring: the consensus that celebrates semiconductor suppliers as the AI buildout’s structural winners and frets over the hyperscalers’ compressing free cash flow accurately describes the present — and is therefore a poor guide to the future. Three frameworks carry the argument.

First, derived demand is a derivative. Gross chip demand comprises net capacity additions plus replacement of the installed base; the expansion term is purchased in proportion to the rate of change of AI capacity, not its level. Because adoption follows an S-curve, that expansion flow peaks near the inflection point — while headline AI demand is still setting records — and declines thereafter even as end demand grows, though the growing installed base raises the replacement floor beneath it. The industry does not need demand to fall in order to suffer; it only needs demand to decelerate, and the amplification chain running from end demand through capex to equipment orders stacks derivatives on derivatives. Second, bottleneck migration: the self-resolving constraint (fabs) crosses the self-intensifying one (power, grid, sites), and rent migrates downstream to the assets a chip glut cannot cheapen. Third, capital dynamics: cash-rich memory oligopolists face a prisoner’s dilemma in which pre-emptive expansion is dominant precisely because no player is financing-constrained, while cash-consuming datacenter operators fight a war of attrition in which exit proceeds in order of capital cost — a war that concentrates the industry around its strongest balance sheets, which then inherit the scarce asset at the moment it becomes the bottleneck. The memory case study binds all three hardest, sharpened by two structural facts: a state-backed fourth player, CXMT, operating under a soft budget constraint, which forecloses the tacit supply discipline that rescued the industry in 2023, and a lead-time asymmetry that makes incumbent expansion threats credible enough to pull forward industry-wide commitment. Three supply waves converge on a 2027–28 window.

The report’s honesty about what it cannot know is as load-bearing as its thesis. The economy’s position on the adoption S-curve is unobservable in real time, so the peak cannot be timed — only monitored, through a dashboard of leading indicators and a trigger-based rotation design rather than a forecast. And the refined thesis that survived the report’s own stress tests is fully conditional: a correction of elevated probability and amplified depth, propagating from the legacy tier toward the premium tier, with the survivors’ market power durable if and only if the binding constraint has by then migrated to power and sites. Every clause conditional; every condition observable. That construction — a structural claim disciplined into a monitoring program — is the series’ method applied to its highest-stakes call.
